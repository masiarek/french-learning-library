# Four inventories: English, French, German and Polish in the IPA

**Level:** 101 · for a reader who speaks Polish or German and is learning French through English books

**One line:** Four languages, one alphabet, four different drawers of it. Of the symbols the dictionaries use, only 14 are in all four languages, all of them consonants. French is closest to Polish in the nasal vowels and to German in the rounded vowels and the r; English is the odd one out, with 15 symbols nothing else has. For a German speaker French has 8 genuinely new sounds, for a Polish speaker 11, for an English speaker 15.

## Why four

The [first lesson](../ipa_english_vs_french/README.md) compared French with English because English dictionaries are where most learners meet the IPA. But a Polish speaker already has nasal vowels, and a German speaker already has /y ø œ/ and the uvular r. The hard sounds of French are not the same for everyone, and a set difference says exactly which they are for each starting point.

## The four inventories

| Language | Key followed | Phonemes | Consonants | Vowels |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| English (RP) | Oxford Learner's Dictionaries | 46 | 24 | 22 |
| French | Le Robert | 36 | 20 | 16 |
| German | Duden *Aussprachewörterbuch* | 44 | 24 | 20 |
| Polish | *Handbook of the IPA*, Jassem's illustration | 36 | 28 | 8 |

Two things stand out before any comparison. English and German are vowel-heavy; German has long and short versions of most vowels and three diphthongs. Polish is consonant-heavy: eight vowels and twenty-eight consonants, among them three whole series of hissing sounds, /s z ts dz/, /ʂ ʐ tʂ dʐ/ and /ɕ ʑ tɕ dʑ/ (*s, sz, ś*), where French has one. French sits in the middle with a large vowel set and a small consonant set.

## What each language has that nobody else has

| Language | Symbols | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| English | /θ ð/, /æ ʌ ɒ/, /ɑː ɔː ɜː/, /dʒ/, /eɪ əʊ ɔɪ ɪə eə ʊə/ | *thin, this, cat, cup, dog, car, saw, bird, judge, day, go, boy, near, square, cure* |
| French | /y ø/ as short vowels, /ɑ/, /ɑ̃ œ̃/, /ɥ/, /o/ | *tu, peu, pâte, dans, brun, huit, eau* |
| German | /ç/, /pf/, /ɐ/, /ʏ/, /ɔʏ/, the long /aː eː oː yː øː ɛː/ | *ich, Pferd, Butter, fünf, neu, Bahn, Beet, Boot, Tür, schön, Bär* |
| Polish | /ɨ/, /ʂ ʐ tʂ dʐ/, /ɕ ʑ tɕ dʑ/, /dz/ | *mysz, szum, żaba, czas, dżem, śnieg, źle, ćma, dźwięk, dzwon* |

The German and French rounded vowels look different only because German writes its tense vowels long: /yː øː/ against French /y ø/. The sounds are near enough that a German speaker reuses them. Likewise Polish /ʂ ʐ/ against French /ʃ ʒ/: Jassem writes the Polish *sz, ż* with the retroflex symbols, Le Robert the French *ch, j* with the plain ones, and a Polish speaker's *sz* is a serviceable French *ch* from the first day.

## What French asks of each speaker

The program subtracts each inventory from the French one. The raw differences are inflated by the symbol choices above, so it subtracts a second time with those allowances made:

| Starting from | New sounds, counted honestly | What they are |
|---|---|---|
| English | 15 | All four nasals, /y ø œ/, /ɥ/, /ɲ/, /ʁ/, and the pure vowels /a ɑ ɛ ɔ o/ |
| German | 8 | Four nasals, /ɑ/, /ɥ/, /ɲ/, /w/ |
| Polish | 11 | /y ø œ/ and /ɥ/, two of the nasals, /ɑ̃ œ̃/, the back /ɑ/, the uvular /ʁ/, the schwa, and the tense /e o/ |

So the German speaker's French problem is almost entirely the nose: the nasal vowels and the glides. The Polish speaker already has the nose, /ɛ̃ ɔ̃/ being *ę* and *ą*, and /ɲ/ being *ń*, and /w/ being *ł*; what is new is the front rounded vowels, the throat r, and the two tense vowels /e o/, which Polish lacks (Polish *e* and *o* are the open /ɛ ɔ/). The English speaker has to learn nearly everything that is not a plain consonant.

## The three marks

| | Stress mark ˈ | Length mark ː | Nasal tilde ̃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| English | yes, stress distinguishes words | yes | no |
| German | yes, same reason | yes, length distinguishes words (*Bahn, Bann*) | no |
| French | no, always the last syllable of the phrase | no | yes |
| Polish | no, always the second-to-last syllable of the word | no | yes |

English and German transcriptions look alike at a glance, French and Polish look alike at a glance, and this table is why. Polish dictionaries do not mark stress because it is fixed; a learner of French who grew up with fixed penultimate stress has one habit to break, moving it to the last syllable, where the English learner has a hundred.

What to read off it:

1. **The core is consonants.** The 14 symbols all four languages share are /p b t d k ɡ f v s z m n l j/. Not one vowel is in all four under the dictionaries' symbols, because English has no plain /a e o/ and Polish no /e o/.
2. **The pairwise table says who is close to whom.** English and German share the most, 26 symbols, being sisters. French and Polish share 23, more than French and English, because of the nasals and /ɲ w/. Polish and English share the fewest, 18.
3. **The two correction lines in section 5 matter more than the raw ones.** German drops from 14 new sounds to 8 once length is ignored; Polish from 13 to 11 once /ʂ ʐ/ stand in for /ʃ ʒ/. A set difference is only as good as the symbol conventions it is fed, so the page says whose conventions it uses.

## How sure is each claim

| Claim | How sure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| The English and French inventories | high | Oxford's key was read for the first lesson; Le Robert's is the standard and from memory |
| The German inventory | high for the sounds, medium for the count | Duden's key from memory; whether /ɛː/ and /ʒ/ count as phonemes varies by description |
| The Polish inventory | high for the consonants, medium for the vowels | Jassem's illustration from memory; the two nasal vowels are often analysed as vowel plus glide, and some descriptions add /ɛ̃ ɔ̃/ only before fricatives |
| Which marks each dictionary uses | high | Oxford, Duden and Le Robert checked in earlier use; Polish dictionaries mark stress only for the exceptions |
