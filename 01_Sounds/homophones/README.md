# Homophones: lit, lie, lis, and the letters French does not say

**Level:** 101 · for anyone who has heard /li/ in a sentence and not known which word it was

**One line:** *Le lit* /lə li/ the bed, *il lit* /il li/ he reads, *la lie* /la li/ the dregs of the wine and *il lie* /il li/ he ties come from four roots, are spelled five ways, and are said one way, /li/. The letters that keep them apart on the page, *-t, -s, -e, -es, -ent*, are the endings of the French verb and the French plural, and they are exactly the letters French does not pronounce; so the ear takes the grammar from the small word before, *le, la, il, ils*, which is why a French verb cannot go without its pronoun the way a Polish one can. A program groups the forms by sound, names the silent letters, counts how many persons of a verb sound alike, and finds where the article and the pronoun settle the reading and where only the sentence does.

## Four roots, five spellings, one sound

| Spelling | The word | Root | IPA |
|---|---|---|---|
| *lit* | *le lit* /lə li/, the bed | Latin *lectus* | /li/ |
| *lit* | *il lit* /il li/, he reads, from *lire* /liʁ/ | Latin *legere* | /li/ |
| *lis* | *je lis, tu lis* /ʒə li, ty li/, I read, you read | Latin *legere* | /li/ |
| *lie* | *la lie* /la li/, the lees, the dregs at the bottom of the wine | Latin *lia*, a Gaulish word | /li/ |
| *lie* | *je lie, il lie* /ʒə li, il li/, I tie, he ties, from *lier* /lje/ | Latin *ligare* | /li/ |
| *lies* | *tu lies* /ty li/, you tie | Latin *ligare* | /li/ |
| *lient* | *ils lient* /il li/, they tie | Latin *ligare* | /li/ |

The near misses, each with a letter that is said:

| Spelling | The word | Root | IPA |
|---|---|---|---|
| *lis*, *lys* | *le lis* /lə lis/, the lily, as in *fleur de lis* /flœʁ də lis/: the *s* is said | Latin *lilium* | /lis/ |
| *lisent* | *ils lisent* /il liz/, they read: the *s* is said, the *-ent* is not | Latin *legere* | /liz/ |
| *lier, lié, liez* | to tie, tied, you tie: the *i* before a vowel becomes /j/ | Latin *ligare* | /lje/ |
| *lions, lisons* | we tie, we read | | /ljɔ̃/, /lizɔ̃/ |
| *lu* | read, the participle of *lire*: /y/ against /i/, the pair of *su* and *si* | Latin *legere* | /ly/ |

Four roots is the count that matters. *Lire* /liʁ/ and *lier* /lje/ look like one family and are not: Latin *legere*, to gather and so to read, and Latin *ligare*, to bind, are two verbs that happened to wear down to the same four letters. The bed, *lectus*, is a third word, and *lia*, the Gaulish word for the wine's sediment, a fourth. Nothing but the spelling remembers which is which, and the spelling is what the ear does not get.

## La lie

*La lie* /la li/ is the sediment of dead yeast that settles at the bottom of a cask or a bottle after fermentation, the lees. A Muscadet *sur lie* /syʁ li/ was left on it over the winter, and says so on the label. Two set phrases keep the word alive: *la lie de la société* /la li də la sɔsjete/ the dregs of society, and *boire le calice jusqu'à la lie* /bwaʁ lə kalis ʒyska la li/ to drink the cup to the dregs, to bear a bad thing to its end. The noun is feminine, and the gender is the one thing the ear gets from the article: *la lie* /la li/ against *le lit* /lə li/.

The verb *lier* /lje/ to tie, to bind, to link gives the same four letters in *je lie, il lie* /ʒə li, il li/, with *tu lies* /ty li/ and *ils lient* /il li/ beside them, and *que je lie* /kə ʒə li/ in the subjunctive. *Lié* /lje/ is the participle: *lié à* /lje a/ linked to, *les mains liées* /le mɛ̃ lje/ with one's hands tied, *se lier d'amitié avec* /sə lje damitje avɛk/ to strike up a friendship with. The vocabulary page of [un lit](../../05_1000_words/lit/README.md) keeps these as table rows, with cards.

## The letters that are not said

Line each spelling of /li/ up under the sound and what is left over is an ending: *-t* in *lit*, *-s* in *lis*, *-e* in *lie*, *-es* in *lies*, *-ent* in *lient*. Those are the person endings of the French verb, *-e, -es, -e, -ent* for *lier* /lje/ and *-s, -s, -t* for *lire* /liʁ/, and the *-s* of the plural noun. Latin said all of them. French stopped saying its final consonants by the end of the Middle Ages and its final *-e* after that, and the spelling, fixed by printers and then by the Académie, kept the letters as they had been. So the written French verb still carries the person in its ending, as Latin did, and the spoken one does not: in the present of *lier* /lje/ four of the six written forms sound alike, in *lire* /liʁ/ and *finir* /finiʁ/ three, in *parler* /paʁle/ four, and even *être* /ɛtʁ/ and *avoir* /avwaʁ/ have a pair each, *tu es, il est* /ty ɛ, il ɛ/ and *tu as, il a* /ty a, il a/.

A Polish verb says who is acting in its ending, *czytam, czytasz, czyta*, and the pronoun stays home unless it is stressed; Latin did the same, *lego, legis, legit*, and Old French still could. Once *lis, lis, lit* all came out as /li/, the ending told the ear nothing, and the pronoun that had been optional became compulsory: *je lis* /ʒə li/, never *lis* on its own. Which change drove the other is argued; that the two go together is the usual account, and it is the reason a learner is told, from the first lesson, that the subject pronoun is never dropped.

## What the word before settles

*Le* /lə/ and *un* /œ̃/ pick the bed, *la* /la/ and *une* /yn/ the dregs: for the two nouns the article is enough, and the article is also all the ear gets of the plural, *le lit, les lits* /lə li, le li/, *un ami, des amis* /œ̃n‿ami, dez‿ami/, or of the feminine, *un ami, une amie* /œ̃n‿ami, yn ami/. A pronoun picks a verb over a noun, *il lit* /il li/ against *le lit* /lə li/. But *il* /il/, *je* /ʒə/ and *tu* /ty/ each leave two verbs open, because *lire* /liʁ/ and *lier* /lje/ share /li/ in the whole singular: *il lit un livre* /il li œ̃ livʁ/ he reads a book and *il lie les deux bouts* /il li le dø bu/ he ties the two ends part only at the object. *Ils* /il/ alone settles them, since *ils lisent* /il liz/ says its *s* and *ils lient* /il li/ does not: the one place in the present where the two verbs part in sound. *Elle lit au lit* /ɛl li o li/ she reads in bed is the sentence to say it all with, three /li/ in a row and no doubt about any of them.

Two kinds of difference are at work, and most sets mix them. One is the silent ending again: *vois, voit*, *peut, peux*, *lu, lue, lus, lues*, *ami, amie, amis, amies*. The other is the several ways French writes one vowel, *ain, aint, ein, eint, eing* for /ɛ̃/, *au, aux, eau, eaux, aut* for /o/, *er, ers, ert, erre, air* for /ɛʁ/; the Pronunciation chapter drills those spellings for [ɑ̃ and ɔ̃](../../04_Pronunciation/sons_an_on/README.md) and for [ɛ](../../04_Pronunciation/son_e_ouvert/README.md). In speech none of this is a problem, any more than *there, their* and *they're* are in English: the sentence settles it before the listener has noticed a choice. On the page it is the whole difficulty of French spelling, which is regular from letters to sound and not from sound to letters.

Three things to read off the output:

1. **Section 2 is the argument in five lines.** Each spelling of /li/ is *li* plus one of the silent endings and nothing else; the five near misses are out of the group because a letter of theirs is said, and *lis* is in both lists, /li/ when it is *je lis* and /lis/ when it is the lily. What you hear is /li/; what you read is the grammar.
2. **Section 3 counts what the ear loses in a verb.** Six written forms of *lier* /lje/ make three sounds, and one of them covers four persons. The written ending carries the person; the sound carries none of it; so *je, tu, il* have to be said.
3. **Section 4 is where to listen.** The article settles a noun, the pronoun settles noun against verb, and the sentence alone settles *il lit* /il li/ against *il lie* /il li/. The one pair the sound does settle is *ils lisent* /il liz/ against *ils lient* /il li/.

## How sure is each claim

The note the owner sent, from an AI assistant, said *la lie*, *le lit* and *il lit* "sound exact or nearly identical" and come from "completely different root words". Both claims hold, the first with the hedge removed. Claim by claim:

| Claim | How sure | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *lit, lis, lie, lies, lient* are all /li/, and *lis* the lily is /lis/ | high | Le Robert's key from memory, and the open ipa-dict word list (fr_FR), read from GitHub on 2026-10-10, which gives /li/ for *lit, lie, lies, lient*, both /lis/ and /li/ for *lis*, /liz/ for *lisent*, /lje/ for *lier, lié, liez*, /ljɔ̃/ for *lions* and /ly/ for *lu* |
| The three are identical in sound, not "nearly" | high for the French of France | one transcription in every dictionary; a Belgian or Swiss speaker may lengthen the vowel before a silent *-e*, *lie* /liː/ against *lit* /li/, as *amie* against *ami*, from memory, medium |
