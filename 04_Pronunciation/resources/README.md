# The best resources: audio and IPA together

A pronunciation resource earns its place when it gives the ear and the eye the same word at once: a native voice, and the transcription in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) beside it. Audio alone leaves you guessing which sound you heard; IPA alone leaves you guessing how it sounds. The lists below are sorted by the five jobs a learner needs done, with a verdict and a how-sure line for each, because most of these sites are blocked from the cloud session that wrote this page and were checked through search summaries or written from memory. Every French word on this page has its IPA after Le Robert’s key, the key of the whole library.

## 1. Look a word up: IPA and audio side by side

| Resource | What it gives | Why it is useful | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Le Robert, dico en ligne ([dictionnaire.lerobert.com ↗](https://dictionnaire.lerobert.com/)) | The transcription in Le Robert’s own key, the key every page here uses, and a recorded voice for a large part of the words; a limited free version, more by subscription | One place to settle a dispute between this library and another tool: the pages follow this key | Medium: blocked from the cloud; a 2009 report counted 16,000 recorded words, the key is known from the print dictionary |
| WordReference, French–English ([wordreference.com ↗](https://www.wordreference.com/fren/)) | IPA for the headword, a recorded voice in several accents (France, Canada and more), free, with a forum where natives answer | The accents side by side are the lesson: one word, two countries | From memory, not checked from the cloud |
| Wiktionnaire ([fr.wiktionary.org ↗](https://fr.wiktionary.org/)) | IPA for nearly every word and inflected form, and recordings by named native speakers with their region (Toulouse, the Vosges, Shawinigan in Québec), collected by Lingua Libre, free | Covers the rare word and the feminine plural that a commercial dictionary leaves out; the region of the speaker is printed | High: entries with IPA and regional recordings were quoted in a search summary |
| Forvo ([forvo.com ↗](https://forvo.com/)) | Recordings only, several speakers per word with sex, region and votes, phrases too; no IPA | Three natives saying one word: the variation you hear is the real language, and the votes say which is standard | High |

## 2. Train the ear: minimal pairs with audio and IPA

| Resource | What it gives | Why it is useful | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| This chapter’s decks, Pronunciation (FR-EN) and (EN-FR) ([the chapter page](../README.md)) | Cards with the IPA under the French, built from minimal pairs such as *blanc – blond* /blɑ̃/ · /blɔ̃/ and *mais – mes* /mɛ/ · /me/; no audio of their own | The pairs are the fastest way to find the one sound you cannot hear; add a voice to every card with the Anki add-on HyperTTS, or record the word from Forvo | High for the decks; the add-on’s name and state from memory |
| Refold, *French Minimal Pairs Trainer* (an Anki deck, paid) | Dozens of French sounds each against a similar sound, *trou – trop* /tʁu/ · /tʁo/, with Standard French audio | A ready-made ear test: hear, choose, turn the card; the author says it pays most after a few weeks of learning | High: the deck page was quoted; about 15 dollars in 2026 |
| Gabriel Wyner, *Fluent Forever* (the book, and the minimal-pair tests on his site and in his app) | The argument for ear training before speaking, and the recipe for making your own minimal-pair cards in Anki | The method behind this chapter: discrimination first, then production; the book is in English and short on the point | Medium: the method is certain, a French trainer in his app was not confirmed by search |
| phonetique.free.fr | A free, old French-as-a-foreign-language site: the phonemes with exercises, discrimination of pairs, liaison, intonation, tongue twisters, with audio | The nasal-vowel discrimination exercises are the ones a book sells; here they are free | Medium: recommended in a teachers’ handout; the site itself was not reached |

## 3. See how a sound is made

| Resource | What it gives | Why it is useful | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seeing Speech, University of Glasgow ([seeingspeech.ac.uk ↗](https://www.seeingspeech.ac.uk/)) | A clickable IPA chart: each symbol plays its sound and shows the vocal tract moving, filmed by MRI and ultrasound or animated | For /y/, /ʁ/ and the nasal vowels, which no English or Polish mouth makes, a picture of the tongue is worth a page of description | High: the project and its charts are documented |
| *Handbook of the IPA*, the French illustration (Fougeron and Smith, *JIPA* 1993) | The official French inventory, with a recording of the fable of the North Wind and the Sun read by a native | The source of the inventory in the [Sounds](../../01_Sounds/README.md) chapter, and the standard against which the dictionaries’ keys are measured | Medium: from memory, the article is certain, the free recording less so |
| Parlez-vous French, Anne Le Grand’s Shorts and her site’s rule pages (this chapter) | One sound per video, the letters that make it in red; the site’s pages on *on, om* and *en, em* | Short enough to repeat ten times; the chapter’s pages carry each video’s words with IPA | High |

## 4. Drill with a course: audio with IPA on the page

| Resource | What it gives | Why it is useful | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Phonétique progressive du français*, CLE International: Débutant, Intermédiaire, Avancé | Each sound set against the one it is confused with, exercises by difficulty, an answer booklet and audio; Débutant has 450 exercises, Avancé (B2–C1) 52 lessons in 4 chapters with 400 exercises and a glossary | The standard classroom workbook; one page per contrast, so a weak sound can be drilled alone | High for Débutant and Avancé (library and publisher records); the Intermédiaire volume from memory |
| *Les 500 exercices de phonétique*, Hachette FLE (Abry and Chalaron): A1–A2, B1–B2 | 500 progressive exercises with answers, in four stages: perception, articulation, rhythm and intonation, spelling to sound with dictation; a summary table of spelling-sound correspondences; audio | The table of correspondences is the roadmap’s “from spelling to sound” lesson in print, and the dictations check it | High (library records, 2010 and 2011); the audio from memory |
| Assimil, *French with Ease* (the owner’s course) | IPA under every sentence of the first lessons, and the recordings | Sentences, not words: the liaison and the rhythm are only there | High |
| YouGlish, French ([youglish.com/french ↗](https://youglish.com/french)) | A word searched in YouTube clips of natives, with large subtitles, clip after clip; free with advertisements; no IPA | A word in a real sentence, with the liaison it gets in speech; the one tool that shows *plus* /ply/ against *plus* /plys/ in context | High |

## 5. Get feedback on your own voice

| Resource | What it gives | Why it is useful | How sure |
|---|---|---|---|
| A teacher: Yosser’s lessons ([the chapter](../../02_Yosser_teacher/README.md)) | A native ear on your own sentences, at once | Nothing else hears the mistake you cannot hear; bring the tables of this chapter to the lesson | High |
| Speechling ([speechling.com ↗](https://speechling.com/)) | Record a sentence after a native speaker; a human coach sends a correction within a day; a free tier run by a nonprofit, unlimited corrections paid | Feedback between lessons, on sentences you choose | Medium: reviews up to 2017 and the app listing agree; the current free tier was not confirmed |
| Your phone’s recorder, then Forvo | Say the word, play the native, play yourself | The cheapest comparison there is; most learners never do it | High |
| Praat ([praat.org ↗](https://www.praat.org/)) | Free phonetics software: record, and see the vowel as two formants on a spectrogram | The program route: your /y/ and a native’s /y/ as two points on a chart, a claim a program can show | High for the tool, from memory for the method |
| Text-to-IPA converters and IPA readers (toipa.org, ipa-reader) | A transcription for any text by rule, and a machine voice that reads IPA aloud | To hear what a transcription you wrote says; the rules break on *examen* /ɛɡzamɛ̃/, *femme* /fam/ and on liaison, so check the dictionary | Medium: toipa.org’s French converter with a voice was quoted; ipa-reader from memory |

## What to use first

1. Every new word: WordReference or Le Robert, for the IPA and the voice together; Wiktionnaire when they lack the word.
2. The sound you cannot hear: a minimal-pair deck, this chapter’s with a voice added or Refold’s, ten minutes a day.
3. The sound you cannot make: Seeing Speech’s chart for the tongue, then Anne’s Short on it, repeated.
4. A book, if one is wanted: *Phonétique progressive*, Débutant.
5. Feedback: Yosser; between lessons, Speechling or the phone’s recorder against Forvo.

## AI assistants for a quick French answer

As of October 2026, from Anthropic’s current model list and from use, not from a benchmark: French is a language the large models have seen a great deal of, so every current frontier model answers grammar and vocabulary questions accurately, and the difference for a *quick* answer is the size of the model, not its maker. In Claude, Haiku 5.5 is the fastest and cheapest, Sonnet 5.5 the best balance of speed and care for everyday questions, and Opus 5.5 or Fable 5.1 the ones to turn to when the question is hard, such as why a rule breaks. Mistral’s Le Chat, from a French company, is fast and strong in French too; ChatGPT and Gemini are fine. On IPA the models are reliable for common words and slip on rare ones and on liaison, so take a transcription from an assistant as a draft and check it in Le Robert or Wiktionnaire, which is what this library does with its own pages. How sure: the Claude line-up is from the current model list; the comparison with other makers is from memory.
